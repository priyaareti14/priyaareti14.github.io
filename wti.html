FORECASTING / FINANCIAL ANALYTICS   /   CASE STUDY 03

Can complex models lose to simple ones?

An honest forecasting experiment where the simplest baseline outperformed a more sophisticated algorithm.

ROLE INDEPENDENT ANALYSTTOOLKIT Python · XGBoost · TableauFOCUS Out-of-time model evaluation
01 / THE BIG QUESTION

If a more sophisticated model is available, should you use it?

I studied WTI crude oil prices alongside financial and macroeconomic indicators to understand short-run associations and test forecasting methods. Rather than reporting an attractive machine-learning score in isolation, I compared a leakage-free XGBoost model against an extremely simple benchmark: next week's price = this week's price.

The analysis uses chronological validation and separates explanatory associations from out-of-sample prediction — two things that are easy to blur.

03 / WHAT I LEARNED

A model isn't better just because it tries harder.

01

A baseline is a business decision tool.

The recent holdout produced $3.15 MAE for XGBoost and $3.08 for persistence. Complexity created no demonstrated forecasting advantage on this test.

02

Time order changes what “good performance” means.

The stricter 2023–2024 backtest further favored the persistence forecast ($2.55 vs. $2.89 MAE). Chronological splits exposed differences that random splitting could obscure.

03

Association is not prediction — or causality.

Adding weekly financial indicators raised explanatory R² only modestly, from ~0.060 to ~0.091. Findings describe historical relationships; they are not causal claims or trading signals.

04 / WHAT I WOULD RECOMMEND
MODELING TAKEAWAY

Choose evidence over complexity.

Use a strong, transparent baseline as the benchmark for any production forecasting proposal. Evaluate out-of-time performance, monitor drift and insist that model complexity deliver a repeatable advantage before adopting it.
