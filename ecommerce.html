PRODUCT ANALYTICS / EXPERIMENT DESIGN   /   CASE STUDY 02

Why are shoppers dropping off?

From a misleading funnel metric to a defensible checkout experiment — with the data quality question asked first.

ROLE INDEPENDENT ANALYSTTOOLKIT SQL · BigQuery · Python · TableauFOCUS Customer journey & experimentation
01 / THE BIG QUESTION

The dashboard said 2.33%.
But was that the right number?

I analyzed the public Google Merchandise Store GA4 dataset to find where customers were falling out of the purchase journey — and what experiment could help. The first strict event-sequence funnel suggested a 2.33% view-to-purchase rate.

Before recommending a redesign, I questioned the definition. Real event paths don't always capture every intermediate action, so I reconstructed and validated the business funnel in BigQuery.

03 / THE REAL BOTTLENECK

The useful answer was hiding downstream.

01

Shipping → payment: ~39% drop-off.

The validated funnel goes from 10,806 shipping-stage sessions to 6,592 payment-stage sessions. This points to a checkout friction hypothesis worth testing.

02

It did not look device-specific.

Desktop shipping-to-payment progression was 60.54%, versus 61.54% for mobile. The available evidence did not justify treating this primarily as a mobile UX issue.

03

An experiment, not an invented uplift.

I designed a 9,552-eligible-user, 50/50 A/B experiment with power calculations, randomization checks and Monte Carlo analysis. No live intervention was deployed; the next step would be an actual controlled test.

04 / WHAT I WOULD RECOMMEND
RECOMMENDED NEXT ACTION

Test checkout friction before rebuilding the entire experience.

Prioritize a focused shipping-to-payment hypothesis, monitor payment completion alongside purchase guardrails, and validate the effect through a properly powered experiment rather than assuming the correlation is causal.
